# Two-Phase Commit (2PC) vs Saga Pattern

*Frontend Team Lead's notes — distributed transactions deep dive*

## Two-Phase Commit (2PC)
- A **synchronous, blocking protocol** for distributed transactions
- A **central coordinator** asks all participants "can you commit?" (phase 1 — prepare)
- Once all say yes, coordinator tells everyone to actually commit (phase 2 — commit)
- Provides **strong atomicity** — all succeed or all fail, like a single ACID transaction spread across systems
- **Downsides:** participants hold locks and wait on the coordinator the whole time → latency, deadlocks, reduced availability. If the coordinator fails mid-process, participants can get stuck holding locks indefinitely.

## Saga Pattern
- Replaces locking with a sequence of **local transactions**, each committed independently
- If a step fails, **compensating transactions** undo prior steps → **eventual consistency** instead of strong atomicity
- No cross-service locking → faster, more resilient, more scalable than 2PC

### Why you can't just "rollback" across services
Rollback works within a single database's transaction because the DB keeps a temporary undo log for uncommitted changes — nothing outside has seen them yet. But once a local transaction **commits**, it's permanently durable (the "D" in ACID), and other things may have already reacted to it (a Kafka event fired, another service read it). There's no shared undo log spanning multiple independent databases — they don't know about each other's transaction state. So Saga doesn't erase the past; it issues a **new, forward-moving transaction** that logically cancels the effect (e.g. "CancelOrder" instead of un-committing "CreateOrder").

*Frontend analogy:* once a `fetch()` call gets a 200 response, you can't "unsend" it. To undo its effect you send a *new* request (e.g. `DELETE`) — you don't rewind the first one.

## Two Ways to Implement a Saga

| | Orchestrated Saga | Choreographed Saga |
|---|---|---|
| **Who's in control** | A central orchestrator explicitly tells each service what to do | No central controller — services react autonomously to events |
| **Traceability** | Easy to audit — one place to see the full flow | Harder to trace — logic is scattered across services |
| **Coupling** | Services depend on the orchestrator | Services stay loosely coupled |
| **Scalability** | Orchestrator can become a bottleneck | Scales better — no single point of coordination |
| **Best for** | Simpler flows where visibility/audit matters | Complex flows with many services, prioritizing decoupling |

*Frontend analogy:* Orchestrated = parent component explicitly calling child functions, controlling the sequence. Choreographed = event bus / pub-sub, where components just listen and react independently with no single owner of the full flow.

## 2PC vs Saga — Full Comparison

| Aspect | Two-Phase Commit (2PC) | Saga |
|---|---|---|
| Coordination | Central coordinator | Choreographed (events) or orchestrated (central saga) |
| Communication | Synchronous | Asynchronous |
| Atomicity | Strong atomicity | Eventual consistency, compensating transactions |
| Flexibility/Resilience | Less flexible, single point of failure | More flexible, resilient, no single point of failure |
| Performance | Slower, participants wait | Faster, independent operations |
| Locking | Involves resource locking → contention | No resource locking, separate local transactions |
| Use Cases | Strong consistency, limited participants | Long-running, complex transactions, multiple services, eventual consistency preferred |

## When to Choose Choreography over Orchestration
- **High scalability needs** — no central orchestrator = no single performance bottleneck
- **Want loose coupling** — services react to events instead of being commanded
- **Complex workflows with many services** — avoids maintaining one giant central state machine

**Trade-off to know:** choreography adds *implementation complexity* (every service needs to understand and react to more event types) and makes tracing a transaction's full journey harder than with an orchestrator's clear audit trail.

## Interview Questions & Answers

**Q: Why is 2PC considered a poor fit for microservices at scale?**
2PC requires every participant to hold locks and wait synchronously on a central coordinator until the whole transaction resolves. In a large microservices system with many services and high request volume, that means locks are held longer, more services are blocked waiting on each other, and the coordinator itself becomes a scaling bottleneck and single point of failure — which directly contradicts the "independent, loosely coupled services" goal of microservices in the first place.

**Q: What specifically causes 2PC to risk deadlocks?**
Because participants hold resource locks while waiting for the coordinator's final decision, if the coordinator is slow or crashes after the "prepare" phase but before sending "commit," participants can be left holding locks indefinitely — blocking other transactions that need those same resources. With multiple concurrent 2PC transactions across services, circular waits on shared resources become possible, producing classic deadlock conditions.

**Q: When would you choose Orchestrated Saga over Choreographed, despite the bottleneck risk?**
When you need clear auditability/debuggability — e.g., regulated workflows (payments, compliance) where you must be able to trace exactly what happened, in what order, and why a transaction failed. The orchestrator becomes your single source of truth for the transaction's state machine, which is worth the coupling trade-off when correctness/traceability matters more than raw scalability.

**Q: How do you debug/trace a transaction in a choreographed saga without a central log?**
In practice, teams solve this with **distributed tracing** (e.g., a correlation ID or trace ID attached to the initiating event, propagated through every subsequent event) combined with centralized logging/observability tools (e.g., Jaeger, Zipkin, or a shared event log in Kafka) that let you reconstruct the full event chain after the fact, even though no single service "owns" the full flow at runtime.

**Q: If eventual consistency is "good enough," why does 2PC still get used anywhere?**
Some domains genuinely need strong, immediate consistency — e.g., financial ledger systems or inventory systems where even a brief inconsistent state is unacceptable, and the participant count is small/known (not hundreds of independent microservices). In those narrower, tightly-scoped cases, 2PC's stronger guarantee is worth its performance cost.
